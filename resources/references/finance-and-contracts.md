# Finance and contracts (`FIN`)

Economics and finance research on buyer power, hold-up, and what customer concentration costs a supplier. This bears on H2, H6 and H7.

Most entries here are **Leads**. They come from the reference list of an external investor brief (see [`../analyses/tyranny-of-the-whale-brief.md`](../analyses/tyranny-of-the-whale-brief.md)), which says every paper was checked to exist. We have verified only FIN-1 ourselves. Read each paper before citing it. Author names are given as initials, as in the brief, until verified.

### FIN-1. Powerful buyers capture value, especially in high-fixed-cost industries

- **Source:** Michael E. Porter, "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy", *Harvard Business Review*, January 2008: <https://hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy> (full-text reprint: <https://public.dhe.ibm.com/software/data/sw-library/cognos/pdfs/articles/art_the_five_competitive_forces_that_shape_strategy.pdf>)
- **Verification:** Verified 2026-09-13 against the reprint PDF.
- **What it says:**
  - "Powerful customers—the flip side of powerful suppliers—can capture more value by forcing down prices, demanding better quality or more service (thereby driving up costs), and generally playing industry participants off against one another, all at the expense of industry profitability."
  - A customer group has negotiating leverage if "There are few buyers, or each one purchases in volumes that are large relative to the size of a single vendor. Large-volume buyers are particularly powerful in industries with high fixed costs…"
  - "High fixed costs and low marginal costs amplify the pressure on rivals to keep capacity filled through discounting."
- **Bears on:**
  - H2 (supports).
  - H7 (supports: the reverse, since many small buyers have little leverage).
- **Used in:** `WHY.md` §3.
- **Caveats:** a strategy framework, not an empirical study of foundries.

### FIN-2. Customer concentration raises a supplier's cost of equity

- **Source:** D. Dhaliwal, J. S. Judd, M. Serfling and S. Shaikh, "Customer Concentration Risk and the Cost of Equity Capital", *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 61(1), 2016, pp. 23–48. DOI 10.1016/j.jacceco.2015.08.003.
- **Verification:** **Lead.**
- **What it says (per the brief):** a concentrated corporate customer base raises both the cost of equity and the cost of debt, and the effect is stronger where the risk of losing a major customer is higher.
- **Bears on:** H2 (supports: concentration makes the next fab more expensive to finance).
- **Used in:** not yet. A strong candidate for `WHY.md` §3.

### FIN-3. Customer concentration worsens loan terms

- **Source:** M. Campello and J. Gao, "Customer Concentration and Loan Contract Terms", *Journal of Financial Economics* 123(1), 2017, pp. 108–136. DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2016.03.010.
- **Verification:** **Lead.**
- **What it says (per the brief):** higher customer concentration increases interest-rate spreads and the number of restrictive covenants, and shortens loan maturities.
- **Bears on:** H2 (supports).
- **Used in:** not yet. A strong candidate for `WHY.md` §3.

### FIN-4. Wal-Mart's suppliers earned lower margins

- **Source:** P. N. Bloom and V. G. Perry, "Retailer Power and Supplier Welfare: The Case of Wal-Mart", *Journal of Retailing* 77(3), 2001, pp. 379–396.
- **Verification:** **Lead.**
- **What it says (per the brief):** Wal-Mart suppliers earned lower profits and gross margins than non-suppliers.
- **Bears on:** H2 (supports, by analogy from retail).
- **Used in:** not yet.

### FIN-5. Concentration can bring efficiencies (a counter-argument)

- **Source:** P. N. Patatoukas, "Customer-Base Concentration: Implications for Firm Performance and Capital Markets", *The Accounting Review* 87(2), 2012, pp. 363–392. DOI 10.2308/accr-10198.
- **Verification:** **Lead.**
- **What it says (per the brief):** concentration can bring coordination efficiencies (lower selling and administrative costs, better asset use, higher accounting returns), even as gross margins fall.
- **Bears on:**
  - H2 (challenges).
  - H7 (challenges): concentration isn't all downside.
- **Used in:** not yet. `WHY.md` §6 should address it once verified.

### FIN-8. Hold-up and relationship-specific investment

- **Sources (Leads):**
  - R. H. Coase, "The Nature of the Firm", *Economica* 4(16), 1937, pp. 386–405.
  - B. Klein, R. G. Crawford and A. A. Alchian, "Vertical Integration, Appropriable Rents, and the Competitive Contracting Process", *Journal of Law and Economics* 21(2), 1978, pp. 297–326. DOI 10.1086/466922.
  - O. E. Williamson, "Transaction-Cost Economics: The Governance of Contractual Relations", *Journal of Law and Economics* 22(2), 1979, pp. 233–261. DOI 10.1086/466942.
  - S. J. Grossman and O. D. Hart, "The Costs and Benefits of Ownership: A Theory of Vertical and Lateral Integration", *Journal of Political Economy* 94(4), 1986, pp. 691–719. DOI 10.1086/261404.
  - O. Hart and J. Moore, "Property Rights and the Nature of the Firm", *Journal of Political Economy* 98(6), 1990, pp. 1119–1158. DOI 10.1086/261729.
  - P. L. Joskow, "Contract Duration and Relationship-Specific Investments: Empirical Evidence from Coal Markets", *American Economic Review* 77(1), 1987, pp. 168–185.
- **Verification:** **Lead.**
- **What they say (per the brief):** when one party sinks capital into assets specific to a relationship, the other party can renegotiate afterwards. A fab tuned to one customer's chips is exposed to this.
- **Bears on:**
  - H2 (supports: lock-in; see CONC-8 on AMD and GlobalFoundries).
  - H11 (context): an open fab that sells general machine time reduces relationship-specific investment.
- **Used in:** not yet.

### FIN-9. Diversification, and pricing each segment separately

- **Sources (Leads):**
  - H. Markowitz, "Portfolio Selection", *Journal of Finance* 7(1), 1952, pp. 77–91. DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1952.tb01525.x.
  - H. R. Varian, "Price Discrimination", in *Handbook of Industrial Organization*, Vol. 1, 1989, pp. 597–654. DOI 10.1016/S1573-448X(89)01013-7.
- **Verification:** **Lead.**
- **What they say (per the brief):**
  - Many small customers smooth demand, the way a diversified portfolio smooths returns.
  - A diversified customer base lets a seller price each segment at its willingness to pay.
- **Bears on:**
  - H7 (supports, from theory).
  - H6 (supports: small, flexible customers can pay more per unit).
- **Used in:** not yet.
- **Caveats:** the portfolio analogy only holds if small customers' demand isn't strongly correlated, for example all falling in the same downturn.
