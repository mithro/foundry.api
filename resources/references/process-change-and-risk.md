# Process change and risk (`RISK`)

Why fabs avoid changing their manufacturing processes. This is evidence for the risk-aversion part of H2.

---

### RISK-1. The industry standard for notifying customers of process changes

- **Source:** JEDEC, "J-STD-046: Customer Notification Standard for Product/Process Changes by Electronic Product Suppliers": <https://www.jedec.org/standards-documents/docs/j-std-046>
- **Verification:** Verified 2026-09-13 for the title and description. The standard itself is behind a login.
- **What it says:** per the fact-check, the standard "establishes the requirements for timely customer notification of changes to electronic products and associated processes". The current revision is J-STD-046A, published December 2025.
- **Bears on:** H2 (context): customers depend on processes staying the same, and changes have to be formally notified.
- **Used in:** `WHY.md` §3.
- **Caveats:**
  - The standard's existence shows that processes are expected to stay stable. It isn't direct evidence of risk aversion.
  - A search summary says the standard requires notice at least 90 days before first shipment of a changed product. That's a **Lead**, from a GlobalSpec page we haven't read.

### RISK-2. Intel's "Copy EXACTLY!" approach to building fabs

- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "Copy Exactly!": <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copy_Exactly!>
  - *Intel Technology Journal*, Q4 1998: <https://www.intel.com/content/dam/www/public/us/en/documents/research/1998-vol02-iss-4-intel-technology-journal.pdf>
- **Verification:** **Lead.**
- **What it says (per search summaries):** new fabs copy the development fab's equipment, chemicals and process details "without changes unless physically impossible", to protect yield.
- **Bears on:** H2 (supports, possibly): the industry's culture treats unchanged processes as essential.
- **Used in:** not yet.
- **Caveats:** this is about moving a process between fabs, not about serving customers. It explains a strong culture of not changing anything, but for yield reasons as much as for customers.

### RISK-3. New automotive chips take years to be approved

- **Source:** Semiconductor Engineering, "Chip Shortages Grow For Mature Nodes", 2021-07-22: <https://semiengineering.com/chip-shortages-grow-for-mature-nodes/>
- **Verification:** **Lead.** A search summary said "it will take as much as five years for the new chips built with 40nm processes to clear validation processes and get put into new vehicles". We didn't find that sentence in the page text we could reach.
- **Bears on:** H2 (supports, possibly): long approval cycles make customers reluctant to accept change.
